Worked example: a single filer with $120,000 of gross income in 2026 has $103,900 of taxable income after the $16,100 standard deduction. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10% ($1,240), the next $38,000 at 12% ($4,560), and the remaining $53,500 at 22% ($11,770), for a total of $17,570. The marginal rate is 22% and the effective rate is 14.6%.

Why the two rates differ: only the income inside a bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. Income in lower brackets keeps its lower rate, so your average rate is always lower than your top rate when your income spans more than one bracket. A raise that pushes you into a higher bracket only taxes the extra dollars at the higher rate.

What this does not include: state income tax, Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, self-employment tax, and tax credits. It always uses the standard deduction, so if you itemize, your tax will differ.
