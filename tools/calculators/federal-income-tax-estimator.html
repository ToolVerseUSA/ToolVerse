Worked example: a single filer with $60,000 of gross income in 2026 subtracts the $16,100 standard deduction to get $43,900 of taxable income. Tax is 10% on the first $12,400 ($1,240) plus 12% on the next $31,500 ($3,780), for a total of $5,020. That is an 8.4% effective rate and a 12% marginal rate, leaving $54,980 after tax.

About the math: taxable income is gross income minus the standard deduction for your filing status and year. Tax is figured bracket by bracket, so only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of taxable income. Your effective rate is total tax divided by gross income.

What this does not include: state income tax, Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, self-employment tax, and tax credits. It always uses the standard deduction, so if you itemize, your tax will differ.

When NOT to use Federal Income Tax Estimator

Questions people ask

What is the standard deduction, and does this calculator use it?

The standard deduction is a fixed amount the IRS lets you subtract from your income before tax is figured. For 2026 it is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, and $24,150 for heads of household. For 2027 it is $16,600 single, $33,200 married filing jointly, and $24,925 head of household. This calculator always uses the standard deduction. If you itemize, your tax will differ.

What is the difference between my marginal rate and my effective rate?

Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of taxable income. Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your total gross income, and it is always lower than your marginal rate when income spans more than one bracket. A single filer with $120,000 of gross income in 2026 has a 22% marginal rate but a 14.6% effective rate.

Does this include state tax, payroll tax, or tax credits?

No. This is a federal income tax estimate only. It does not include state income tax, Social Security or Medicare payroll taxes, self-employment tax, or any credits such as the child tax credit. It also assumes you take the standard deduction and have no other adjustments.

Where do the bracket figures come from?

Yes. The 2026 figures come from IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32, and the 2027 figures come from IRS Revenue Procedure 2026-35, published November 5, 2026 with news release IR-2026-133. The tax rates are the same in both years: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%.

Sources

2026 brackets and standard deduction, IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. 2027 brackets and standard deduction, IRS Revenue Procedure 2026-35, published November 5, 2026 with news release IR-2026-133. Rates in both years are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%.
